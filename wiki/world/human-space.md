# Human Space

*Established Day 4, in conversation between Kristoff and Voss aboard Rusty; galactic geography and coordinate convention added ooc the same day.*

**Star charts map the galaxy relative to Sol** (Earth's sun), divided into concentric, Greek-lettered "rings" exactly 4,167 light-years apart. **Ring Alpha** starts at Sol itself — more a sphere than a true ring, since it's the innermost division — and rings count outward in sequence toward a theoretical **Ring Omega** at the absolute edge of the main galactic disk, 100,000 light-years out. Rings are technically spherical shells, but everything ever catalogued lies within about ±5,000 light-years of the galactic equator, hence the name. **Ring Omicron**, roughly 62,500 light-years from Earth and well past the galactic core into the far-side disk, is the outermost ring catalogued to date. Full coordinates give an azimuth in degrees (0°/360° points from Sol toward Sagittarius A*), an elevation in light-years off the galactic equator (positive toward Earth's north, negative south; degrees ±90° used instead outside the main disk), then the ring letter and a light-year offset from that ring's boundary — e.g. `12.2213°+2.8671 β4100.2281`.

**Central Command** sits in Ring Alpha, at the Earth–Sun L2 Lagrange point — close enough to dead center of Ring Alpha to round off as such.

The *Kestrel* was operating in Ring Omicron, at the very edge of catalogued space, when she was lost — why a survey vessel was that far from home is not yet established. That distance is why the numbers don't work: Ring Omicron had long since dropped off her long-range scanners, to say nothing of Sol and Ring Alpha further in. A directed SOS to Central Command needs a rough bearing to aim along; from here, there isn't one to give. Broadcasting blind at the power a subspace signal would need is a different problem entirely: the energy required is orders of magnitude beyond anything the *Kestrel* could have produced at full output, let alone anything salvageable from her wreck by hand. No fusion reactor, no comparable power source, exists anywhere the crew has found.

Standard protocol for a survey vessel that misses its scheduled check-ins does exist: eventually Central Command flags the silence and runs a search cone off the last reported vector. Both Kristoff and Voss agree this is real but not meaningful on any timescale either of them expects to live to see — years before a search even starts, light-years of "plausible" after that.

Voss's assessment, given without argument: no ship, no signal, no realistic path home. The working priority has shifted from *get home* to *live well here, as long as we get to* — which he still considers something he can command.

**Status:** both crew members treat rescue as effectively closed for practical planning purposes, though not absolutely zero. Kristoff later raised one real caveat: a missing survey vessel last logged surveying a class D anomaly would draw a *specific* search to that anomaly's coordinate eventually — standard procedure, not wishful thinking. Voss agreed the procedure is real but downgraded the hope anyway: likely months out at best, no guarantee a search crew investigates thoroughly rather than scanning and logging the *Kestrel* as debris, no guarantee Central risks more than a handful of ships into a location that has already swallowed one, and no guarantee a rescue ship wouldn't suffer [the same fate](../locations/the-kestrel.md) rather than bring one. Filed as "not zero, not soon, not something to plan around today."
